    How an appeal actually works

    1. Internal appeal. You (or an authorized representative) ask the insurer to reconsider. For most employer and Marketplace plans, federal rules give you at least 180 days from the denial notice to file. The insurer must decide urgent cases within 72 hours, pre-service cases typically within 30 days, and post-service claims typically within 60 days.
    2. Get the file. You are entitled to your full claim file and the criteria the insurer used. A denial that can't show its criteria is a weak denial.
    3. External review. If the internal appeal fails, an independent reviewer outside the insurer can overturn it — and that decision binds the insurer. In California this is the Department of Managed Health Care's Independent Medical Review (IMR), which is free.
    4. Deadlines are the whole game. Miss the internal-appeal window and most later rights shrink or disappear. That is why this checker starts with the date.

    Questions

    Is this legal advice?

    No. Overturned provides general information about appeal rights, deadlines, and published statistics. It is not legal or medical advice and does not create an attorney–client or doctor–patient relationship. For advice about your specific case, consult a licensed attorney or your treating clinician.

    How sure are you about my deadline?

    The date shown is computed from the standard federal rule for your plan type (for example, the 180-day internal-appeal window for most employer and Marketplace plans, or 60 days for Medicare Advantage reconsideration). Some plans and states allow more time. Treat the date here as the earliest safe deadline and confirm with your plan. If your computed date has passed, appeal anyway — late appeals are sometimes accepted for good cause, and external review or a regulator complaint may still be open.

    Why do so few people appeal?

    The Commonwealth Fund's 2025 survey found about 1 in 5 privately insured adults had a denial in the past year, and only about half of those with a claim denial appealed. KFF found fewer than 1% of denied in-network ACA claims are appealed. Yet appealed denials are overturned at high rates — 43–67% of appealed prior-authorization denials in KFF's analysis of 2025 data. The system counts on people giving up.
